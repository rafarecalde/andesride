Central Quito sits high. The figure travelers repeat — about **9,350 feet**, or about **2,850 meters** — is the usual description of the city, and it is the one to plan around. You will notice it. Most people notice it as ordinary shortness of breath, not as an emergency. A few people feel rotten on night one. Neither outcome is something you can predict from how fit you are at sea level.

This is practical travel guidance, not medical advice. If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant, or have been told to be careful at altitude, ask your own clinician before the trip. If you feel seriously unwell after you arrive, get medical care rather than waiting out a blog's tips.

## The airport is lower than the city you are driving to

Mariscal Sucre (UIO) is in Tababela, in the valley east of Quito. The terminal is lower than the historic center and the modern city up the hill. Cumbayá, on the valley path between the airport and town, is also lower than central Quito. The drive into the city is a climb, which is why the first stairs at the hotel can feel harder than the walk through the terminal.

We are not publishing a separate elevation for the runway or for Cumbayá here. Sources differ by a few hundred feet depending on which point they measure, and a precise number is not what your lungs use. The planning fact is enough: the city is the high part, the airport and the valley are the easier first night if you want one. Published elevations are worth a recheck if you are comparing neighborhoods for a medical reason. For everyone else, "about 9,350 feet in the city" is the right round figure — the same 2,850 meters already used in our [layover guide](/guide/quito-layover-guide).

The drive times that go with that climb are in [how far the airport is](/guide/how-far-is-quito-airport-from-the-city): about 45–60 minutes to Quito, 25–35 to Cumbayá, 10–15 to the airport hotel.

## What people actually feel

On a normal arrival, exertion is the tell. A staircase, a hill in Old Town, or walking fast with a suitcase can leave you breathing harder than the same effort would at home. Some travelers get a headache, sleep badly the first night, notice a dry mouth, or feel light-headed if they stand up quickly. Alcohol hits harder.

That cluster is common enough that you should not fill the arrival day. It is also mild enough, for most healthy visitors, that a quiet evening and a normal night of sleep are the whole treatment. Severe trouble breathing at rest, confusion, a headache that is not ordinary, or symptoms that frighten you are a reason to seek care — not a reason to take a walk and see.

The equator makes this feel contradictory. Quito is not cold in the way a ski town is cold, and the sun is strong. You can be warm in a sunny plaza and short of breath on the walk back. UV is intense at this latitude and this elevation. A hat and sunscreen matter even when the air feels cool, as the [Mitad del Mundo](/guide/visiting-mitad-del-mundo) note mentions.

## First-night habits that are worth keeping

- Drink water. Cabin air plus altitude is a dry combination. Have water in the car and at the hotel.
- Keep the first day easy. A hotel check-in, a simple meal, and an early night beat a scheduled hike.
- Go easy on alcohol the first evening. The view can wait until tomorrow.
- Walk slowly in the historic center. The streets are steep even before you count the elevation. The [Old Town guide](/guide/quito-old-town-guide) is the right companion, after you have slept.
- Save the big altitude jumps for later in the trip. The [TelefériQo](/guide/teleferico-quito-pichincha) and a [Cotopaxi](/guide/cotopaxi-national-park-day-trip) day are rewarding and they are higher than the city. They are a poor program for the afternoon you land.

If you want the gentler first night on purpose, sleep in [Cumbayá](/guide/quito-airport-to-cumbaya-transfer) or at the airport hotel, then go up to Quito after a night's rest. That is a preference, not a requirement. Plenty of people ride straight to Old Town and feel fine.
